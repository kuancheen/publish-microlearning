The Case for a Harder, Deeper Life

💡 Core Takeaway

True satisfaction comes not from avoiding strain, but from building a life anchored in specific place and purpose.

Wendell Berry died at age 92 in Port Royal, Kentucky, where he farmed using traditional techniques and wrote in a cabin without electricity. His life offers a counter-narrative to the modern pursuit of frictionless convenience. In 1964, at age 29, Berry made a radical decision to leave a promising academic career at New York University to return to his family’s farm in Henry County, Kentucky. His supervisor warned him that this move would amount to "intellectual death," citing Thomas Wolfe’s famous line that "you can’t go home again." Berry’s face was set like flint; he stayed. He believed he could not write accurately about "people and place" from afar, stating, "The landscape was there to check me if I was wrong."

In an era defined by digital numbness and the avoidance of cognitive strain, Berry’s example is urgent. We often use technology to escape discomfort, turning work into performative theater and politics into safe, distant radicalism. Berry rejected this. He understood that hardness and strain are not enemies to be evaded. When oriented toward an intentional vision, these challenges yield a foundation that withstands the storms of human existence. His life demonstrates that the great satisfactions of living come from rebuilding your life into something deeper, rather than smoother.

Try it today: Identify one area of your life where you are currently avoiding necessary friction—whether it is a difficult conversation, a complex task, or a physical challenge. Spend five minutes writing down why you are avoiding it and what specific, small step you can take today to engage with it directly.
🤔 Where in your life are you currently trading depth for convenience?

Source: Cal Newport, "Wendell Berry and the Promise of the Deep Life"
